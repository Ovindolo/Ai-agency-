# Agency Addendum — Unit Economics

Interactive model: `08-unit-economics.html` · https://claude.ai/artifact/SEcdpfj5FhwsLpQLXQXWZ2

The campaign quotes no CPA target, and shouldn't — nobody can before an impression runs. But the **ceiling** is derivable before launch, and leaving the media buyer without one is an abdication dressed up as rigor. This derives it.

**Every input is an agency assumption.** No supplier has quoted, no order has shipped, no customer has repeated. The model's value is its structure and its sensitivities, not its defaults.

## Four findings

### 1. Correction — the refill is not "the business." It is half of it.

Earlier in this engagement the agency wrote that *the refill is the business.* At these assumptions that is wrong. The starter kit contributes $17.61 unaided — **54% of twelve-month value.**

This is a structurally *stronger* position than a loss-leader model: survival does not depend on retention. It also means acquisition can be judged on first-order economics, which is a far faster feedback loop than waiting on a repeat cohort — and it changes how Test 1 should be read.

### 2. Lever — fulfillment is the second-largest cost on the starter.

At $5.40 it is **15.9% of the starter price**, more than processing and returns combined. The free-shipping threshold sits at $30 (see F-05), so the $34 kit always absorbs it.

Raising the threshold above the starter price would recover margin and kill conversion. The real lever is **packaging the starter to ship at letterbox rate** — which is a product decision, not a marketing one, and it belongs in the brief for the physical bottle before tooling is committed.

### 3. Decision — the subscription discount has a hurdle rate.

A 15% subscribe-and-save cuts refill contribution from $18.62 to $14.68. To be worth offering, it must lift refill volume by at least **26.8%.**

That is testable, not a matter of preference. Test it before the discount is printed on the page — a discount is far easier to launch than to withdraw.

### 4. Gap — nothing in this package earns the refill. `[F-14]`

**46% of twelve-month value arrives after the ad has done its job, and the campaign specifies no post-purchase communication at all.** No refill reminder timed to the bottle running out. No first-use email improving the odds the product actually works for them. No replenishment trigger.

Seven organic posts were specified for people who have not bought, and zero touches for people who have. That is a real hole in the work, found by modeling it rather than by reading it.

**What it needs, at minimum:**

| Timing | Touch | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Day 0 | Order confirmation + the honest test | *"Don't judge it while it's damp. Spray tonight, check tomorrow."* Sets the evaluation the product can win |
| Day 3 | First-use follow-up | The two-spot technique — waistband and back of the knee. Most product failure is application failure |
| Day 21 | Usage check | One question, no offer. Surfaces the refill objection before it becomes silence |
| ~Day 100 | Replenishment | Timed to assumed bottle life. **This is the single highest-leverage message the brand will ever send** |
| Lapsed +60d | Win-back | One attempt, then stop |

Five emails. They carry 46% of the value, and they were missing.

---

## How to make this decision-grade

1. Get three supplier quotes. COGS confidence goes from Low to High in a week.
2. Weigh the packed starter and price parcel vs. letterbox properly.
3. Ship 100 units. Measure the real return rate.
4. Wait one bottle cycle and measure the real repeat rate — then delete the assumption.

Until then this is a structure, not a forecast. Nothing in it predicts performance and no output should be quoted as a projection.
