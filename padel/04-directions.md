# Phase 3 — Three Directions

Three different engines: a **problem**, a **place**, and a **product**. Pick one. Everything downstream is built from it.

---

## DIRECTION A — "DEAD GRIP"

**The idea.** Name the thing every regular player is already doing wrong and has decided not to think about. You are playing on a dead grip right now. You know it. Here is why that costs you more than you think.

**Emotional angle.** Recognition, then mild embarrassment, then relief. Not aspiration — correction.

**Hook.** *"Your grip died two weeks ago."*

**Visual world.** Forensic. Macro on a worn grip: the shine where the palm sits, the frayed edge at the butt cap, sweat darkening, the wrap gone loose. Next to it, a fresh one. Hard directional light. Almost clinical, which in a category of neon sports photography is the differentiating move.

**Content engine.** Enormous. *"Post your grip"* is an instant participation format — every player has a bad one and knows it. Grip-condition guides, how long yours should really last, what a dead grip does to your elbow.

**How it sells the subscription.** Perfectly. The problem is forgetting; the product is not-forgetting. The logic goes in a straight line from hook to offer.

**Main advantage.** It works on a stranger in two seconds, it makes a low-stakes problem feel specific, and it argues for the subscription rather than bolting it on.

**Main risk.** Nagging. Get the tone wrong and it is a brand that tells you off. And the underlying problem is genuinely low-stakes — nobody's week is ruined by a slick grip.

---

## DIRECTION B — "THE CLUB"

**The idea.** Grips made for the people at your club, distributed through clubs, with the club's name on the box if they want it. Not a tennis brand's leftovers repackaged for padel.

**Emotional angle.** Belonging. Padel is social before it is competitive — people play with the same four people every week.

**Hook.** *"Ask at the desk."*

**Visual world.** Documentary. Real clubs, real courts, Spanish and Italian light, phone-shot energy. Faces, not products. The opposite of A.

**Content engine.** Club takeovers, player features, a club-of-the-month, co-branded packs. Genuine local partnership content.

**How it sells the subscription.** Weakly on its own. Clubs sell singles at the desk. Subscription has to be introduced separately.

**Main advantage.** **It matches the economics exactly.** Contribution is €13.64 — too thin for cold paid, but a club conversation that moves 50 units costs nothing but time. This is the only direction whose channel is already affordable.

**Main risk.** Slow, relationship-dependent, and you are in Romania trying to build relationships with clubs in Spain. It is the direction most likely to work and least likely to work *fast*.

---

## DIRECTION C — "THE ONLY PART YOU TOUCH"

**The idea.** You spent €300 on a racket and €2 on the only part of it your hand ever makes contact with. Treat the grip as equipment, not as tape.

**Emotional angle.** Craft. Quiet superiority. Equipment-nerd satisfaction.

**Hook.** *"€300 racket. €2 grip."*

**Visual world.** Premium macro. Texture, weave, thickness in cross-section. Black, matte, tactile. Product-as-object photography of a thing that costs three euros — which is the joke and the point.

**Content engine.** Moderate. Material explainers, thickness and tack comparisons, why perforation matters. Good saves, low shares.

**How it sells the subscription.** Badly. Craft positioning argues for buying one good thing, not for recurring delivery.

**Main advantage.** **It justifies a higher price**, which is the direct fix for the AOV problem. It is also the most brandable and the best-looking of the three.

**Main risk.** It is a claim we cannot yet prove. Asserting superior feel before a sample has been held is exactly the kind of thing this agency does not do. And it competes with racket brands on the ground they already own.

---

# Recommendation

## Lead with **A — "Dead Grip"** as the message, deliver it through **B — "The Club"** as the channel.

That is not a hedge, and here is the distinction: **A is what the advertising says. B is where it gets said.**

**Why A is the message.** The brief's core insight is that the gap between *knowing* and *having* is the business. Direction A is that insight said out loud. It also does the thing this product most needs — it makes the case for subscription as a logical consequence rather than a discount mechanic. Nobody subscribes to grips because they love a brand. They subscribe because they are tired of forgetting.

**Why B is the channel.** The economics decided this, not taste. At €13.64 contribution we cannot buy cold traffic reliably, and pretending otherwise would burn your €300 to prove a thing the spreadsheet already said. A club conversation that moves fifty units costs a phone call. **The campaign has to live where the money works.**

**Why C is rejected as the lead, despite being the best-looking.** It requires us to claim the grip is better before anyone has held one, it argues against the subscription that carries the economics, and it picks a fight with Bullpadel and Head on their own turf. C's discipline survives as a **product standard** — the grip must actually be good, or A becomes a brand that criticises your grip and then sells you a worse one. But "it feels nice" is not a campaign a stranger cares about.

**The honest catch in this recommendation.** B is slow, and you are building it remotely from Romania. If club outreach stalls, we are left with A's message and no affordable channel — at which point the answer is a bigger first order (the season pack) to make paid viable, not more patience. That fallback belongs in the plan now, not discovered in week six.

---

## STOP — approval gate

**Phase 4 does not start until you pick.** Say A, B, C, or something else entirely. Naming, identity, packaging and every asset get built from whichever you choose, and switching afterwards means rebuilding rather than editing.

## Meanwhile — five questions for your padel contact

Free research that replaces guesswork. Their answers go straight into the brief, and I would rather have one real player's words than my own invention.

1. **How often do you change your overgrip — honestly, not ideally?** And how do you decide it is time?
2. **Where do you buy them, and what is annoying about that?** Club desk, Decathlon, online, or borrowing off someone?
3. **What makes a grip good or bad in your hand?** Tack, thickness, sweat, how long it lasts?
4. **Do you have a brand you buy, or do you take whatever is there?** *(If the answer is "whatever is there," that is the opening confirmed.)*
5. **Would your club stock a small brand's grips, and who decides that?**

Ask them in their own words and send me the answers unedited. Do not clean them up — the way a player actually talks about this is the copy.
