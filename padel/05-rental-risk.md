# Signal — The Rental Problem

**Source:** primary, and **partially mis-classified on first logging — corrected below.**

The user's brother-in-law reports that Romanian clubs rent racket and equipment for **~15 RON (≈€3) for 3 hours**, and that buying your own gear is therefore not worth it.

> ### CORRECTION
>
> This document originally described the source as *"an active padel player in Romania."* **He is a tennis player** who occasionally plays padel, considers it boring, and says he would never trade tennis for it.
>
> That changes the weight of his evidence substantially. A tennis player who already owns tennis equipment and does not rate padel will obviously rent rather than buy padel gear. **That is not evidence about how padel players behave.** It is evidence about how a tennis player behaves in a sport he does not intend to take up.
>
> The agency over-updated on a single data point from outside the target segment. Logged as a research error, not a market finding.

**Date logged:** before any money was spent.

## His second claim, checked

He also said that **nobody who plays tennis goes to padel.** That is a factual claim and it is checkable. The data contradicts it:

- *"Many players come from tennis or squash backgrounds, but around half are new to racket sports entirely"* `[VERIFIED]`
- *"Roughly 58% of active US padel players also play pickleball or tennis regularly"* `[VERIFIED]`
- Core padel demographic: adults **26–50**, and about **40% women** — a high female share for a racket sport `[VERIFIED]`

Tennis players do cross over, in large numbers. His view is a real attitude held by some tennis players; it is not a description of the market.

**And it is beside the point anyway.** We do not need tennis players to convert. We need people who *already play padel* to buy grips. Padel has 19.4 million players whether or not this particular tennis player approves of it.

## Why the rental point still matters, at reduced weight

**A player who does not own a racket never buys an overgrip.** There is no version of this product that reaches a renter. The entire thesis assumes racket ownership as a precondition, and that assumption was never tested — it was inherited from the category, not verified.

**The comparison is brutal.** Renting a complete setup for three hours costs about the same as **one overgrip at retail** (€2–4). For a casual player in Romania, ownership is not merely unattractive — it is economically irrational. Our product sits on the far side of a purchase decision that the market is actively pricing people out of making.

**It also breaks the market sizing.** Every number in `01-product-decision.md` counts *courts* and *players*. Neither is the addressable market. The real one is:

> players who own a racket **and** play often enough to wear grips out

Nobody publishes that number. `[VERIFIED absence — searched, no published ownership rate exists]`

## The honest reading

**This does not kill the thesis. It exposes the load-bearing unknown underneath it.**

One data point, from Romania — a market already ruled out for selling — from a **tennis player who does not play padel seriously.** That is close to no evidence at all about the target segment.

But it is a real warning, for a reason that goes beyond one person:

**If a meaningful share of people who look like padel fans in ad targeting are renters, then paid acquisition is partly buying impressions from people who structurally cannot buy.** At €13.64 contribution there is no room to absorb that. It would not show up as "the ads don't work" — it would show up as a CAC that never comes down no matter how good the creative is, which is the most expensive way to learn something.

It also, incidentally, **strengthens the club-channel recommendation**: at a club you can physically see who walks in carrying their own racket.

## The process note — revised

The first version of this document treated one conversation as a serious threat to the thesis. The follow-up showed the source was outside the segment, and the threat shrank accordingly.

**Both moves were the same error: letting one person's opinion stand in for market data.** Over-updating on inconvenient evidence and then under-updating when it turns out to be convenient are the same failure wearing different clothes.

What survives untouched is the actual gap: **nobody has told us what share of regular padel players own a racket.** That number was unknown before this conversation and it is unknown now. One tennis player's opinion moved it neither up nor down.

So the test below stands exactly as written. It was the right response to the original signal and it is the right response now — and I am not cancelling it just because the evidence that prompted it got weaker. The number still decides the business.
